# **Hybridisation**

## **Hybridisation Theory**

Let's look at the structure of $\mathrm{CH_4}$

<div id="ch4-tetrahedral-viewer"></div>

The carbon atom forms 4 single bonds, one with each of the 4 hydrogen atoms.

Since there are 4 bond pairs and 0 lone pair, the shape w.r.t the carbon atom is **tetrahedral** and the bond angle is **109.5 degree**.

Based on what we have learned so far, we can confidently deduce that these 4 single bonds consist of 1 s-s and 3 p-s $\sigma$ bonds between the carbon atom and the 4 hydrogen atoms.

However, the s orbital and the three p orbitals of carbon are arranged in such a way that the p orbitals are **perpendicular** to one another, with angles of 90 degree between them. If that is the case, **how can methane have a tetrahedral shape with bond angles of 109.5 degree?**

Linus Pauling proposed that valence atomic orbitals of atoms in a molecule are different from those isolated atoms.

In the hybridisation theory, **atomic orbitals (s and p in H2 chemistry) mix to form new orbitals called hybrid orbitals which can involve in $\sigma$ bonding.**

Hybridisation of orbitals results in hybrid orbitals with the same shape, size and energy level (degenerate)

## **$\mathrm{sp^3}$ Hybridisation**

Let's look at $\mathrm{CH_4}$ again

In $\mathrm{CH_4}$, the carbon atom forms **4 $\sigma$ bond** and it has a **tetrahedral** shape.

Therefore, the carbon atom in $\mathrm{CH_4}$ is **$\mathrm{sp^3}$ hybridised**

**1 s** and **3 p** orbitals mix to form **4 $\mathrm{sp^3}$ hybrid orbitals**. These 4 hybrid orbitals are arranged in tetrahedral shape.

## **$\mathrm{sp^2}$ Hybridisation**

Let's look at $\mathrm{CH_2O}$

In $\mathrm{CH_2O}$, the carbon atom forms **3 $\sigma$ bonds and 1 $\pi$ bond** and it has a shape of **trigonal planar**.

Since it needs to form a $\pi$ bond, it needs to **reserve 1 p orbital**.

Thus it is **$\mathrm{sp^2}$ hydridised**.

**1 s and 2 p orbitals mix to form 3 $\mathrm{sp^2}$ hybrid orbitals**. These 3 hybrid orbitals are arranged in trigonal plannar shape and the unhybridised p orbital is perpendicular to the hybrid orbitals.

## **$\mathrm{sp}$ Hybridisation**

Let's look at $\mathrm{CO_2, CHN_3}$

In both molecules, the carbon atom forms **2 $\sigma$ bonds and 2 $\pi$ bonds** and the molecules are linear.

Since it needs to form 2 $\pi$ bond, it needs to **reserve 2 p orbital**.

Thus it is $\mathrm{sp}$ hydridised.

**1 s and 1 p orbital mix to form 2 $\mathrm{sp}$ hybrid orbitals**. These 2 hybrid orbitals are arranged in linear shape and the unhybridised p orbitals are perpendicular to the hybrid orbitals.

## **Hybridisation and strength of covalent bond**

$\mathrm{sp^3}$, $\mathrm{sp^2}$ and $\mathrm{sp}$ orbitals have **different length/size** due to different amount of **p character**.

$\mathrm{sp^3}$ orbital is the **longest / most elongated** as $\mathrm{sp^3}$ orbitals come from mixing 1 s and **3** p orbitals. $\mathrm{sp^2}$ orbital (_1 s and 2 p orbital_) is the second most elongated and $\mathrm{sp^3}$ orbital (_1 s and 1 p orbital_) is the least elongated.

Since bond length affects strength of covalent bonds, $\mathrm{sp^3}$ orbital forms the **weakest** bond, followed by $\mathrm{sp^2}$ and $\mathrm{sp}$ orbitals

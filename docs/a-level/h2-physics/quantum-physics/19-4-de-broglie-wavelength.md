## **de Broglie Wavelength**

Matter particles can have wave behaviour. The wavelength associated with a particle is called its de Broglie wavelength.

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p}
$$

For a non-relativistic particle:

$$
p = mv
$$

Therefore:

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}
$$

## **Why Electrons Show Wave Behaviour**

Electrons have very small mass, so their momentum can be small enough for the de Broglie wavelength to be comparable to atomic spacings. This allows observable diffraction.

Everyday objects have much larger momentum, so their de Broglie wavelengths are extremely tiny and diffraction is not noticeable.

## **Worked Example: Electron**

An electron has speed $7.3 \times 10^6\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{(9.11 \times 10^{-31})(7.3 \times 10^6)}
$$

$$
\lambda = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}
$$

This is similar to atomic spacing, so diffraction is possible.

## **Quick Comparison: Macroscopic Object**

A $0.050\ \text{kg}$ ball moving at $10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ has:

$$
\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{(0.050)(10)}
$$

$$
\lambda = 1.3 \times 10^{-33}\ \text{m}
$$

This wavelength is far too small to observe diffraction in everyday situations.

!!! warning "Common misconception"
    A larger speed does not always mean more obvious wave behaviour. The wavelength depends on momentum, so large mass makes the wavelength extremely small.
